Part 1

The place

Where the animals lived, and what “near a road” means on the ground.

Northern Washington, close to the Canadian border. Between 2018 and 2022 researchers fitted GPS collars to bobcats and coyotes at two sites about 150 km apart.

Each collar logged a position about every four hours. The 70 animals give roughly 117,000 usable positions.

Amber is bobcat, blue is coyote.

Zoom in on the Okanogan site. The pale lines are every road a vehicle can use, from highways to forest tracks.

One coyote, MVCOY58M, followed for 634 days. The green band is everything within 250 m of a road, drawn to scale. The dashed outline is roughly where it lived.

It was shot in April 2021. A lot of its territory sat inside the green band. This page asks whether that goes with being killed by people.

Part 3

Living near roads

How we measure “near a road”, and what it shows.

First, the idea. A collar gives positions on a map.

A movement model turns the positions into a map of probability: how likely the animal is to be in each square. Darker means more time. The squares add up to 100%.

Now mark every square within 250 m of a road.

Add up the probability inside the marked squares. That total is the animal's road exposure.

A simplified example with 100 m squares, not a real animal.

Now every animal. Each dot is one animal. The higher it sits, the more of its territory lies within 250 m of a road.

Shape and colour show how each animal ended.

Look at the coyotes killed by people. Most sit high.

The dash marks the middle (median) of each group: 67% for coyotes killed by people, against about 48% for the rest. That is roughly 18 points higher.

The vertical lines show how sure we can be. They are explained next.

Shownfor coyotes

Bobcats show a smaller gap, and the uncertainty is too wide to call it real.

Suggestedfor bobcats: no clear gap

Change how exposure is measured

Where do the vertical lines come from? Take the 14 coyotes killed by people. These are their exposures.

Draw 14 coyotes at random from that group, allowing repeats, and find the median. Darker dots were drawn more than once.

Do that 2,000 times. Each bar counts how often a median landed in that range.

The middle 95% of those medians is the interval. A wide interval means the answer would swing a lot with a different handful of animals.

This method is called the bootstrap.

Part 4

Does more road mean more risk?

Relating exposure to the yearly chance of being killed by people.

Each tick along the bottom is an animal, placed by its road exposure. A diamond marks one killed by people.

The line is a model: the yearly chance of being killed by people, at each level of exposure. It rises.

The band shows how far that line could plausibly move.

Another view of the same band: each frame is one of 1,000 refits on a random draw of animals. They scatter widely.

A flat line, meaning no effect, fits inside the band. The data lean towards more risk with more road, but cannot settle it.

Do bobcats and coyotes share ground? Score a pair of territories from 0 to 1. Two territories far apart score 0.

Partly overlapping territories score in between.

Identical territories score 1.

The score is the Bhattacharyya coefficient: how much probability two maps share.

Now the real animals at one site. Each square is a pair. Darker means more shared ground; grey means the pair was never collared together for 90 days.

Is a bobcat–coyote pair more or less overlapped than two of the same species? The average gap is 0.006. To judge that, shuffle the species labels at random 2,000 times and see what chance produces. The grey band is that range, and the observed gap sits well inside it.

Suggestedno difference found, which is not proof there is none

Shared ground is not contact in time: animals tracked in different years may share space without ever meeting.

Summary

The four findings

Each carries the same confidence tag as where it appeared.

What we can say, and what we can't

The data show

  • People caused most recorded deaths.
  • Coyotes killed by people lived closer to roads.
  • Bobcat deaths by people came in winter.

The data suggest

  • Risk rises with road exposure, but uncertainly.
  • Bobcats and coyotes do not overlap unusually.

The data cannot say

  • Why: wolf and cougar locations are not public, so the “fear of large carnivores” explanation is untested.
  • Whether animals meet: overlap is in space, not time.

Method notes

Territories were estimated with continuous-time movement models and autocorrelated kernel density estimation (ctmm). The risk model, group medians with bootstrap intervals, the overlap shuffle test, the explainer diagrams and the maps run live in your browser; the movement models, range-weighted exposure and pairwise overlap values were precomputed in R and Python. Uncertainty marks are 95% intervals except the risk-curve band (90%). The public data hold 70 animals; the source paper reports 72.

Data: Movebank, GPS tracking of bobcats and coyotes in northern Washington (Prugh et al. 2023, Science 380:754–758; doi 10.5441/001/1.gm93267b, CC0). Roads: © OpenStreetMap contributors. Layout inspired by the ONS article “Exploring local income deprivation”. Species colours (amber bobcat, blue coyote) follow the Okabe–Ito colour-blind-safe palette; outcomes also differ by shape.
